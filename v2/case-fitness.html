How it came together

Designing an AI that is allowed to be wrong safely

The coaching engine is not a single prompt. A master-coach persona sets voice and boundaries; feature-specific prompts handle training, nutrition and recovery; and a second model reviews every output before it reaches a user. In a health-adjacent product, that validation layer is the difference between helpful and hazardous. To test something this open-ended, the team built its own harness: a synthetic-user generator that simulates ten weeks of coaching, with three competing models grading the AI's decisions. Errors surface in simulation rather than in someone's actual training week.

Conversation instead of forms

People abandon fitness apps because logging is work. Here, a user writes "I ate borscht without meat" and the system estimates calories and macros on its own. Workouts are rescheduled and targets adjusted in the same chat. The interface asks less and infers more — which is what keeps the data complete enough for the AI to be useful. Underneath, the language model is swappable by configuration, so the product is not tied to a single vendor's roadmap or pricing. In parallel, a dedicated legal specialist assembled a 52-document register covering terms of use, health disclaimers and privacy — the unglamorous work that decides whether a health product can actually launch.
