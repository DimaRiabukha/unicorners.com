How it came together

Three modules, one verdict

The service is built from a text-to-phoneme converter, a duration predictor and a tempo corrector. Original and translated text are each converted into a sequence of phonemes for their own language; an AI model estimates speech duration from those phonemes; and the tempo correction logic adjusts the translated duration to align with the original recording. The relative difference between the two is then calculated and analysed to produce a recommendation: use it, or send it back for manual rephrasing. The API takes the texts, the original duration and an acceptable difference, and returns a decision — which is what makes it usable as an automated gate rather than a report someone has to interpret.

The details that decide whether it works

Two problems turned out to matter more than the model itself. The first was punctuation: every symbol carries a duration of silence that changes the length of a spoken phrase and therefore its tempo. The team researched phrases of varying length across languages, punctuation symbols, and the position of those symbols within a sentence. The second was numbers. Applying text-to-speech directly to digits produced inconsistent results, because the same numeric format can be voiced several different ways. The fix was to convert numeric data into its written form before synthesis — 18.08.1996 becomes August eighteen, nineteen ninety-six — so the duration prediction matches what a voice will actually say.
