Wrapmate solved printing and installation. Design is the part that still moves at human speed.

The company already does the hard logistics well: premium 3M vinyl, a satisfaction guarantee, and the largest network of certified installers in the country — more than two thousand of them — so a customer approves a design and it gets fitted locally. The bottleneck sits earlier, at the moment a business owner is trying to picture what their van could look like.

A vehicle wrap is not a flat graphic. It is a surface that bends around door seams, handles, mirrors and compound curves, and it is different for every make, model and year. A layout that looks balanced in a design file can put a logo across a door gap or lose half a phone number around a wheel arch. That is why design has stayed manual, per vehicle, per customer.

What an AI design tool would actually have to do

Generating a nice-looking image is the easy half. The tool has to generate a design that is manufacturable: aligned to the real template for that vehicle, aware of where the panels split, and exportable as print-ready files an installer can work from without redrawing anything.

The proposed system starts from what a business already has — logo, colours, typeface, the message it needs on the side of a vehicle — and produces wrap concepts laid onto the exact template for the chosen make and model. The customer sees it on their own vehicle in 3D, iterates in plain language, and approves. What leaves the tool is not a picture but a production file.

For commercial programmes the same engine solves a second problem: one identity applied consistently across a mixed fleet, so a sprinter van, a pickup and a compact car read as the same brand rather than three separate attempts at it.

What the community would build
