They shipped the AI fast. Then their customers’ auditors started asking questions the team could not answer.

The client runs a multi-tenant SaaS platform that sits directly against its customers’ production databases — the tooling DBAs and platform teams use to monitor, query, optimise and govern them. Their markets are finance, healthcare and enterprise SaaS: all of them with auditors attached.

On top of that platform they had launched an AI assistant with real authority: an index and optimisation advisor that recommends schema changes, natural-language-to-SQL that generates and runs queries, and anomaly detection that flags abnormal database behaviour. Shipping it fast left the team exposed on three fronts at once.

They could not prove it was correct. The assistant sometimes generated SQL that ran cleanly but returned the wrong data, or referenced columns that did not exist. There was no baseline to measure against and no way to catch quality slipping after a model update.

They could not prove it was safe. An LLM able to perform database actions is a new attack surface. A crafted prompt might coax it into reaching data outside a user’s permissions, or across tenant boundaries. Their existing security testing had been built for the classic application.

They could not prove it was compliant. Enterprise buyers’ auditors were asking how AI decisions are logged, traced and governed — and with EU AI Act enforcement landing in August 2026, deferring the answer had stopped being an option.

Why more tooling would not have closed it

They had already bought scanners and monitoring. A scanner finds known CVEs; it does not decide what to test on a non-deterministic model, build a golden dataset, or recognise a prompt-injection path specific to one schema and one set of business rules. A monitoring platform shows a dashboard; it does not produce a defensible ship / don’t-ship verdict, and it does not remediate what it finds.

And no tool maps findings to the EU AI Act, SOC 2 or GDPR, attaches governance context, or assembles an evidence pack an external auditor will accept. The three problems interact: an AI correctness bug becomes a data-leakage incident becomes a compliance gap — precisely the failures that testing each surface in isolation misses. What the client needed was not another tool to run themselves, but one team to own all three surfaces and hand back proof.

Three workstreams, one evidence trail

01

NL-to-SQL semantic correctness

Schema-aware validation of whether the generated query means what the user actually asked — not merely whether it executes.

02

Golden-dataset benchmarking

A curated set of prompts mapped to expected intent and result sets, giving the model a fixed accuracy reference instead of a moving target.

03

Hallucination & drift gates

Checks for invented columns, tables and joins, plus a regression pipeline that re-runs the golden set on every model update and blocks releases that regress.

04

Classic security surface

Black/grey/white-box penetration testing across application, APIs, infrastructure and cloud, secure code review, and RBAC and multi-tenant isolation verification.

05

AI attack surface — red team

Prompt injection, jailbreaks and guardrail validation, data leakage through the AI layer, and model abuse — mapped against the OWASP LLM Top 10.

06

Audit-ready compliance

Gap analysis against the buyer’s actual regulator, an audit-trail review for AI decisions, and governance context attached to every finding by a certified specialist.
